How it works

Every frame from your camera goes through a real-time implementation of Eulerian Video Magnification (Wu, Rubinstein, Shih, Guttag, Durand & Freeman — MIT CSAIL, SIGGRAPH 2012), rebuilt as a WebGL2 pipeline. There is no server. There is no model. It is signal processing on your GPU.

  1. 01

    Decompose

    Each frame is converted to YIQ and split into a Gaussian/Laplacian pyramid — a stack of blurrier and blurrier copies. Tiny changes that are invisible per-pixel become measurable once neighbouring pixels are pooled.

  2. 02

    Filter in time

    Every pixel of every pyramid level runs through a temporal band-pass filter: two first-order IIR low-passes whose difference isolates just the frequencies you care about — 0.7–2.5 Hz for a pulse, 0.15–0.8 Hz for breathing. All of it in float textures, ping-ponged on the GPU.

  3. 03

    Amplify & collapse

    The band-passed signal is multiplied by α (up to ×150) — clipped per level using the paper's spatial-wavelength rule so motion doesn't tear — and added back onto the full-resolution frame. Blood volume changes the reflectance of skin by a fraction of a percent; ×60 makes it a visible flush.

  4. 04

    Measure the pulse

    Separately, the mean colour of skin pixels in the oval is tracked over ten seconds and run through POS (Wang et al., 2017) — a projection that cancels lighting and specular changes — then a band-pass, a Hann window and a 2048-point FFT. The dominant peak is your heart rate; its signal-to-noise ratio is the confidence bar.

Privacy

The page makes no network requests after it loads. Camera frames live in GPU memory and are overwritten thirty times a second. Recordings and snapshots are written straight to your downloads. Open the network tab and watch nothing happen.

Not a medical device

Remote photoplethysmography from a consumer webcam is typically within a few beats per minute of a chest strap in good light, and much worse in bad light, with motion, or with heavy makeup. It is a toy that happens to be real science. Don't make health decisions with it.

Things to point it at

Your own face (Pulse). A sleeping pet or a baby from across the room (Breathing). A washing machine, a speaker, a guitar string, a bridge you're standing on, a phone on a table with the fan running (Vibration). The camera itself must be still — prop the phone up.
